Teaching Design in the Age of the Thinking Machines

What AI reveals about creativity, authorship, and the future of design education.

Author
Azim Afifi, Lecturer, The One Academy
Written for
MIID [issue / date]
Reading time
About 8 minutes

First day of semester and I run the same icebreaker I have run for years: Two Truths and a Lie. Simple enough, come up with three statements about yourself and I must guess which one is the lie.

I gave them five minutes. Walked around the room, and that is when I noticed it.

Several students had their phones out. Not scrolling but actively typing into an AI chatbot. Asking it to help them come up with facts about themselves. I froze.

This is not a question on design, not a research task, not even a creative challenge. It is the simplest cognitive act: reflecting on your own life and selecting three things from it. There is no formula to follow. There is only you, and what you know about yourself. And they still could not do it without assistance.

Kahneman would call this a failure of System 2 to engage: the slow, deliberate and effortful thinking that reflection requires. Instead, the default was obvious: reach for the tool and outsource the discomfort.1

As I walked around the room, I remembered Descartes. Cogito, ergo sum. I think, therefore I am.2 Descartes was not making a claim about intelligence. He was making a claim about existence. Thinking is not a feature of the human being; it is the basis of the human being. It is what makes you real.

So, I found myself asking a question: if we delegate the thinking, what remains?

Evolution of Learning

Education was not always about content delivery. The Socratic method operated on a completely different logic: learning as structured questioning, designed to lead the student toward insight they already held within, buried under their assumption. The teacher was not a repository; he was a provocateur. The student was not a receiver; he was a thinker.3

That model did not scale and education industrialised. Knowledge became curriculum. Curriculum became content. Freire called it the "banking model" where teachers deposit information and students store it. The goal shifted from forming thinkers to producing graduates. The shift was slow enough that we mostly did not notice.4

Then the internet accelerated it. Then Google made retrieval instant. And now AI has completed the circle: students can outsource not just the finding, but the thinking itself.

Postman warned us about this decades ago. Every technology, he argued, does not merely assist culture, it restructures it.5 It eliminates the practices that came before it. We did not just add calculators to mathematics education. We quietly changed what mathematics education was for.

The same restructuring is happening now in design education.

What Design Education is Actually For

Interior design is not a discipline that rewards the accumulation of information. It rewards judgment. The ability to read a space, read a client, read a context, and then make a series of decisions that are coherent, intentional, and defensible. That capacity does not come from a prompt. It comes from practice. From being wrong. From having to explain why.

Zumthor describes architectural knowledge as slow, embodied, rooted in specific material memory: the smell of a particular wood, the sound of a particular floor, the way light falls in a room you loved as a child. He is describing a form of knowing that cannot be generated. It must be lived.6

Pallasmaa extends this further. Design has become too visual, too concerned with the render, the output, and in doing so has lost the sensory and existential intelligence that makes spaces matter to those who inhabit them. The eyes alone, as he writes, do not build a home. The whole body does.7

Now we have a tool that produces beautiful visuals instantly. Coherent layouts, plausible concepts, and a generation of students who are increasingly unable to tell you why they made the choices they made because they did not make them.

Sennett writes about craftsmanship as ethical development, not just technical development. The act of making something slowly, carefully, with full ownership of every decision builds something in the person doing it. Not just skill but character. The willingness to sit with difficulty rather than bypass it.8 Design education, at its best, is the same thing. It is not about producing a portfolio. It is about producing a designer. Those are not the same outcome.

Authorship and What We Need to Teach

Arendt distinguished between labour, work, and action. Labour is recurring and necessity driven. Work produces a durable object. Action is where identity is formed, where the self enters the world and makes a claim. Design, at its best, is action. It is a statement about how the world should be arranged, for these particular people, in this particular place, at this particular moment.9

When a student asks AI to generate their concept and refine it and present it, they have not acted in Arendt's sense. They have supervised a process. That is not design authorship. That is something else. I am not saying AI has no place in design education. I use it myself, for research, for quick visualisation tests, for identifying gaps or playing devil's advocate. But there is a meaningful difference between using a tool to extend your thinking and using it to replace thinking.

Schön described professional expertise as "knowing-in-action": tacit, embodied knowledge that cannot be reduced to a formula. The reflective practitioner navigates situations that have no right answer, because they have internalised enough experience to make judgment calls under uncertainty.10

That is the designer we are supposed to be producing. That kind of knowing only develops through struggle. Not suffering for its own sake, but genuine cognitive effort. The productive difficulty of not knowing and having to find out.

It means we design assessments around the reasoning, not just the outcome. A crit that only evaluates the final render is measuring the wrong thing. A crit that asks the student to walk you through every decision (the why, where, when, who and how) is measuring the right thing. AI cannot fake that conversation. Not convincingly, at least not yet.

The prompt is not the design. The judgement about which output to develop, which to discard, and what the output is still missing, that judgement is the design.

It means we teach students to use AI the way a senior designer would: as a fast sketchpad, not a creative director. And it means we talk openly about authorship. About what it means to say, "this is mine." Design is one of the few professions where your name goes on the wall. Students should understand what that means before they put it there.

Tell Me Something True

I still run Two Truths and a Lie at the start of every semester, but I give them explicit instruction to put their phone face down. No AI. Just you and what you already know about yourself.

Some of them genuinely freeze. Not because they have nothing to say, but because they have not been asked to retrieve something from inside themselves in a very long time. That pause is the work. Kahneman's System 2 engaging. The slow, deliberate thinking that reflection demands. It feels inefficient. It feels like doing nothing. It is, in fact, the thing we are here to develop.

Descartes placed thinking at the centre of existence. Not producing, generating or optimising. Thinking. Slow, uncertain, sometimes circular, occasionally wrong, but always yours.

As design educators, that is what we are protecting. Not from technology. But for our students, because it is theirs to lose, and they may not realise it until it is already gone.

Notes

  1. Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011). Kahneman describes two modes of cognition: System 1, which is fast, automatic, and instinctive; and System 2, which is slow, deliberate, and effortful. Reflection and genuine self-knowledge require System 2. The default drift toward AI assistance is, in Kahneman's terms, a systematic avoidance of System 2 engagement. ↩
  2. René Descartes, Discourse on the Method (1637). The proposition locates the self in the act of thinking rather than in any external attribute. It is the foundational proposition of Western philosophy and carries weight in an age of cognitive outsourcing. ↩
  3. Plato, Meno and The Republic. Socratic method operates through structured questioning designed to expose contradiction and lead the student toward reasoned self-discovery. ↩
  4. Paulo Freire, Pedagogy of the Oppressed (1968). Freire's "banking model" describes the student as a passive receptacle for deposited knowledge, rather than an active constructor of meaning. ↩
  5. Neil Postman, Technopoly: The Surrender of Culture to Technology (1992). Postman argues that technology does not merely assist culture, it restructures it, often eliminating the cognitive practices that preceded it. ↩
  6. Peter Zumthor, Thinking Architecture (1998). Zumthor describes design as a slow, sensory, embodied practice rooted in specific knowledge and personal memory. ↩
  7. Juhani Pallasmaa, The Eyes of the Skin: Architecture and the Senses (2005). Pallasmaa argues that the dominance of the visual in contemporary architecture has severed design from the fuller sensory and existential intelligence that great buildings possess. ↩
  8. Richard Sennett, The Craftsman (2008). Sennett makes a sustained argument for skilled, slow, embodied making as a form of ethical and cognitive development. ↩
  9. Hannah Arendt, The Human Condition (1958). Arendt distinguishes between labour, work, and action, where action is the domain of identity formation and public meaning-making. ↩
  10. Donald Schön, The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action (1983). Schön describes professional expertise as "knowing-in-action": tacit, embodied knowledge that resists reduction to explicit rules or formulas. ↩
