The Learning Floorplan

A concept I've been developing: planning corporate training the way an interior architect plans a floor. Illustrated below with a live example from my own teaching.

Type
Concept
Domain
Corporate L&D, onboarding
Status
Seeking a pilot partner

Interior architects and learning designers solve the same problem: a person arrives with one understanding of a space, and must leave with another. Buildings do it with walls and light. Training programs do it with content and time. The tools transfer better than either profession realizes.

The premise

Most corporate training is planned like a syllabus: a list of topics divided by available hours. Interior architects never plan that way. They plan for a body moving through space: what it sees first, where it slows down, what it remembers after leaving. The Learning Floorplan applies four spatial principles to program design.

The four principles

  1. Sightlines. In a well-designed floor you always know where you are and where you are heading. In a well-designed program, learners can see the whole journey from day one: what they will be able to do, and how each session builds toward it.
  2. Circulation. Architects choreograph movement: compression before release, corridor before atrium. Programs need the same pacing: dense input followed by open practice, never six hours of corridor.
  3. Thresholds. Crossing a doorway changes behavior; psychologists call it the doorway effect. Programs need deliberate thresholds too: rituals that mark the shift from listening to doing, from novice to practitioner.
  4. Dwell points. Every good floorplan has places designed for lingering. In learning, dwell points are where retention happens: structured pauses where learners apply, discuss, and make the material their own before moving on.

A worked scenario

The setup: a four-week elective, Storytelling Workshop, with no prior fixed structure or assessment weighting. Not a corporate program, but the closest live test this concept has had so far, run ahead of finding a corporate pilot partner.

Sightlines: before session one, students got a "Skill Tree": five badges building toward a three-tier perk ladder, laid out in full so the whole four-week arc was visible from day one instead of revealed week by week.

Dwell points: session one closed with a structured three-question feedback poll, a deliberate pause built into the session rather than an afterthought. Nine responses in four minutes: participation 4.44/5, clarity 4.56/5.

What the poll didn't catch: the scores read well, but in the room, engagement looked different: little eye contact, closed body language. The gap between the two readings mattered more than either number alone. The lowest-clarity response named the badges as the reason they took part at all, which reframed the read from "nervous with poor delivery" to "nervous with nothing prepared," and changed how session two was run.

This is one cohort, in one elective, not a corporate program. A pilot with an L&D team is the next real test, at a different scale, and it's what this page is asking for.

What I would measure

  • Retention at 30 and 90 days, tested on application rather than recall
  • Manager-observed behavior change against the program's stated outcomes
  • Learner-reported orientation: "I knew where the program was taking me and why"
